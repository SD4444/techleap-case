The challenge

Herbicides pollute the water. Replacing them raises costs for farmers and puts crop performance at risk.

The water authority's view

Herbicides reach surface water through spray drift and runoff.

Water authorities are responsible for water quality but do not control how farms manage weeds. They can monitor, advise and co-fund alternatives, but at the end of the day, it's the farmer that chooses the method.

Deep dives
The grower's view

Weed control must work on time and protect crop economics.

Functional technology alone is not enough. It must clear weeds on time, avoid crop damage and make economic sense. Until that is proven at commercial scale, herbicides remain the lower-risk choice. Failure can reduce current yield and increase future weed pressure.

Evidence the market can be organised

The building blocks already exist.

Public institutions already fund agricultural water-quality measures, established programmes reach growers, and agricultural sectors report a need for temporary transition support.

40,000 hectares already engaged

For more than twenty years, Schoon Water voor Brabant has linked the province, water boards, Brabant Water and agricultural organisations. Around 500 farmers and contractors participate across 40,000 hectares; 110 receive individual guidance. This demonstrates institutional reach, not committed demand.

Source: Schoon Water voor Brabant ↗

The government's 2025 review reports that the inulin-chicory sector is developing primarily mechanical weed-control techniques but still needs temporary transition support. Herbs and witlof growers cite high labour costs and worker shortages. This demonstrates an adoption barrier, not willingness to pay.

Technology landscape

Two robotic approaches can reduce reliance on herbicides.

Both approaches use cameras and machine vision to distinguish crops from weeds. The difference is how they kill the weed: physical removal or targeted light.

Physical removal

Principal advantages
  • No herbicide at the point of treatment
  • Immediate and observable weed removal
  • Based on established mechanical principles
Principal constraints
  • Performance varies with soil and moisture conditions
  • Tools can disturb soil or damage crop roots
  • Wear, maintenance and repeated passes affect cost

Targeted optical energy

Principal advantages
  • No herbicide at the point of treatment
  • Can target weeds close to the crop
  • Does not disturb soil or crop roots
Principal constraints
  • Requires reliable identification and precise targeting
  • Treatment speed and energy use determine field capacity
  • Dust, rain, plant cover and weed size can affect performance

From technical capability to commercial alternative

The ability to identify and kill a weed is only the starting point.

To become a commercial alternative, a robot must pass five tests. These determine whether growers will buy, manufacturers can plan production and investors will fund scale-up.

  1. Consistent control of the relevant weeds in the target crop
  2. No material crop damage or yield loss
  3. Enough hectares treated within the operating window
  4. A competitive total cost per hectare, including repeat passes
  5. Reliable operation, maintenance and seasonal support

Each crop and technology combination must be validated separately. Success in one crop does not qualify the machine for another. The programme can expand wherever performance and farmer economics are proven.

Each actor waits for evidence another must finance.

Growers want proven field economics. Technology companies need capital to build the machines that generate that proof. Investors hesitate without binding demand. The evidence needed to unlock both demand and capital therefore remains unfunded.

Coordination failure: economic agents fail to align their decisions, leaving everyone worse off even though a mutually beneficial outcome is possible.

The missing asset is not another expression of interest.

It is underwritable demand: identified buyers, committed budgets, defined volumes and measurable performance conditions.

The intervention

Clean Weeding Advance Market Commitment

The programme combines conditional purchases with reserved public funding. Before suppliers compete, it defines volumes, performance standards, buyer contributions and the maximum public contribution. Suppliers set their own prices.

01 · Commit buyer demand

Buyers state what they will purchase.

Growers and agricultural contractors sign conditional commitments specifying machine volumes, target crops, delivery periods and maximum upfront contributions. Contractors may buy machines to serve several farms.

02 · Reserve the public contribution

Reserve a capped contribution for each qualifying purchase.

A water board, potentially supported by the province, reserves a capped contribution. It is paid when the buyer purchases a machine that has passed validation.

03 · Publish the performance standard

Set common requirements, not prices.

Publish requirements for efficacy, crop safety, field capacity, seasonal reliability, maintenance and delivery. Suppliers set their own prices; the programme sets neither the machine price nor a maximum cost per hectare.

04 · Validate and activate

Passing validation activates the purchase.

Independent testing confirms whether the machine meets the published standard. If it passes, the conditional purchase becomes binding on the agreed terms. If it fails, the purchase is not activated and no purchase payment is due.

Deep dives

The purchase mechanism

A purchase activates only when the farmer is better off.

Independent validation must show that the robot maintains or improves crop performance and lowers the farmer's total weed-control cost.

Validation condition 1Yield and crop margin ≥ current method
Validation condition 2Total farmer cost < current method

The farmer contribution must be affordable and economically justified.

The farmer's contribution is agreed before validation through structured interviews and a binding offer. It must leave the farmer with a clear economic benefit from switching, through lower weed-control costs, higher crop margin or both.

Investor relations

Move investor relations from networking to transaction support.

Organise investor engagement around live financing cases. Each interaction should advance diligence or identify the evidence blocking investment.

01 · Define investor requirements

Define investor requirements before designing the pilot.

Interview relevant deep-tech investors and production financiers to establish the evidence they require on demand, field performance, manufacturing costs, unit economics, margins and delivery capacity.

02 · Present each case consistently

Present every financing case in a consistent format.

For each financing case, present the same core evidence: conditional purchases, buyer contributions, the validation plan, production requirements, unit economics and delivery plan. Add field results as they emerge, distinguish evidence from assumptions and state the remaining risks.

03 · Convene investors around decisions

Replace broad networking with milestone-based engagement.

Convene investors when commitments are ready and capital is needed for machines and validation. Reconvene after validation for production capital. Record who enters diligence, requests more evidence or declines, and why.

  1. Investor requirements shape the pilot from the start.
  2. Investors see what is proven, what is assumed and what still requires funding.
  3. Each discussion ends with a next step or a documented reason for declining.

Techleap improves the quality of the investment decision.

Techleap does not select investments, negotiate terms or promise capital. It coordinates the process, standardises the evidence and shows companies what still prevents investment.

Months 1 to 2 · Map the opportunity

Map technologies against the crops and weed-control operations they can serve. Interview growers, contractors, technology companies, water institutions and investors. For each case, identify the affected farms and waterways, the responsible public institution and the overlap between:

  • A documented herbicide-related water problem
  • A crop and operation the technology can address
  • Grower interest and viable farm economics
  • A suitable field-testing window
  • Investor requirements and appetite for financing machine construction and validation

Several combinations may proceed.

Months 2 to 4 · Design and test the commitments

With growers and contractors, establish affordable contributions and purchase conditions. With the public funder, confirm the maximum contribution and legal route, including procurement and state-aid compliance. Agree:

  • How each machine will be tested
  • The required performance
  • The buyer contribution
  • The maximum public contribution
  • What happens if the machine passes or fails

Test the proposed terms with investors: would they assess financing machine construction and validation against these commitments, and what further evidence or conditions would they require?

Months 3 to 6 · Sign, finance and prepare validation

Where buyers, suppliers and public funders agree the terms, sign the conditional purchase commitments. Technology companies use those commitments to seek investment for machine construction and validation. Begin field trials when financing, machines and crop cycles allow; otherwise, narrow, redesign or stop the case.

The thesis

The Vredepeel field trial

The lower-chemical system required more labour and produced lower margins.

From 2020 to 2023, Wageningen researchers compared two arable farming systems on sandy soil at Vredepeel: a conventional reference system and an Integrated Crop Management system.

The ICM system combined preventive measures, monitoring and targeted treatment to reduce dependence on chemical crop-protection products.

What the study found

Across the full crop rotation, yields were 6% lower under ICM on average. After additional hand-weeding costs, contribution margin per hectare was 16% lower. Weed control in fine-seeded crops was the principal bottleneck.

What the result means

The ICM system sharply reduced, but did not eliminate, the use of crop-protection products targeted for substitution. The trial shows the gap that lower-chemical weed control must close: comparable weed control and crop performance without excessive labour or lower margins.

Scope note

This was one four-year farming-system trial on sandy soil. Its results do not represent every crop, farm or lower-chemical method.
