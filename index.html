The challenge

Herbicides pollute the water. Removing them costs growers yield and profits.

The water authority's view

Herbicides reach surface water through spray drift and runoff from treated fields.

Water authorities are responsible for regional water quality, but they do not decide how weeds are controlled on farms. They can monitor, advise and co-fund cleaner practices; the purchasing decision still sits with the grower. The authority carries the water-quality obligation without controlling the switch.

Deep dives
The grower's view

Weed control has to work within narrow operating windows and meet the crop's economic profile.

For a grower, promising technology is not enough. It must clear weeds on time, avoid crop damage and make economic sense. Until that performance is proven at commercial scale, herbicides remain the lower-risk choice. A failed treatment can cost yield today and increase weed pressure tomorrow.

Technology landscape

Two robotic approaches can reduce reliance on herbicides.

Robotic weeders use cameras and machine vision to distinguish crops from weeds, then treat individual plants. The main technical difference is how they kill the weed: physical removal or targeted optical energy.

Physical removal

Principal advantages
  • No herbicide at the point of treatment
  • Immediate and observable weed removal
  • Based on established mechanical principles
Principal constraints
  • Performance varies with soil and moisture conditions
  • Tools can disturb soil or damage crop roots
  • Wear, maintenance and repeated passes affect cost

Targeted optical energy

Principal advantages
  • No herbicide at the point of treatment
  • Can target weeds close to the crop
  • Does not disturb soil or crop roots
Principal constraints
  • Requires reliable identification and precise targeting
  • Treatment speed and energy use determine field capacity
  • Dust, rain, plant cover and weed size can affect performance

From technical capability to commercial alternative

The ability to identify and kill a weed is only the starting point.

To become a credible alternative to herbicide-based weed control, a robotic system must demonstrate all five. Performance against these measures determines whether growers can order with confidence, manufacturers can plan production and investors can finance commercial scale-up.

  1. Consistent efficacy in the target crop and against the relevant weeds
  2. No material increase in crop damage or yield loss
  3. Sufficient field capacity within the available operating window
  4. A competitive total cost per hectare, including repeat passes
  5. Reliable operation, maintenance and technical support throughout the season

Each actor waits for evidence that another must finance.

Growers will not commit at scale until field performance and economics are proven. Technology companies cannot prove them without financing and building commercial machines. Investors hesitate while customer demand remains non-binding.

The evidence that would unlock demand and capital can only be produced through deployments that neither side will finance independently.

Coordination failure: when economic agents fail to align their decisions, leaving everyone in a suboptimal or worse-off state even though a mutually beneficial outcome is possible.

The missing asset is not another expression of interest.

It is underwritable demand: identified buyers, committed budgets, defined volumes and measurable performance conditions.

The intervention

Clean Weeding Advance Market Commitment

The programme combines conditional private purchases with reserved public funding. It establishes the volume, performance requirements and price conditions before robotic-weeding suppliers compete.

01 · Commit private demand

Growers and contractors state what they will buy

Before a supplier is selected, growers and contractors sign conditional agreements to buy or lease machines, or to purchase robotic weeding as a service.

Each agreement states the crop, number of machines or hectares, delivery season and maximum amount the buyer will pay.

The agreement takes effect only if a supplier passes the field test.

02 · Reserve the public contribution

Water institutions fund the temporary environmental premium

The water board sets aside a fixed budget. The province may provide additional funding.

This money covers the verified difference between the cost of robotic weed control and what growers currently pay, up to a pre-agreed limit. It is released only for suppliers that pass the field test.

The public contribution falls as the technology becomes cheaper and more productive. It ends once robotic weed control reaches the agreed cost of the grower's current method.

03 · Publish the commercial standard

Every supplier competes against the same requirements

Before suppliers apply, the programme publishes one set of commercial requirements.

These cover weed-control performance, crop damage, hectares treated per day, seasonal reliability, replacement of the target herbicide application and maximum cost per hectare.

Any supplier that meets all the requirements can qualify.

04 · Validate and activate

Passing the field test activates the contracts

An independent field trial tests each supplier against the published requirements.

If a supplier passes, the conditional purchase, lease or service agreement takes effect. The buyer pays the agreed amount; the public programme pays its approved contribution.

The supplier can then show investors contracted revenue, rather than expressions of interest, when raising capital for production.

Deep dives

The purchase mechanism

A purchase activates only when the farmer is better off.

Independent field validation must show that the robot maintains or improves crop performance while reducing the farmer's total cost of weed control.

Validation condition 1Yield and crop margin ≥ current method
Validation condition 2Total farmer cost < current method

Unverified yield improvements are valued at zero.

A reasonable farmer contribution completes the commitment.

The farmer signs a conditional order stating the amount they can reasonably invest upfront. That contribution is limited by both the verified economic value the machine creates for the farm, and the cash the farmer can invest without borrowing or weakening farm operations.

The technology company sets the machine price. The farmer commits an affordable upfront amount, and the public fund covers the remaining amount up to a fixed programme cap.

The machine must pass the agreed field-performance tests before the purchase is activated. If the required public contribution exceeds the programme cap, the purchase does not proceed.

Once the tests are passed, the technology company receives the full purchase price at delivery.

Research and decision gates

Five decisions before launch.

Each gate must produce a concrete answer. Several can run in parallel, but all five must be resolved before the programme commits funding.

  1. Target use case

    Select the crop, weed-control operation and catchment. Confirm that the relevant herbicide contributes to a documented local water-quality problem and that robotic technology could replace its use.

  2. Farmer business case

    Verify current weed-control costs, yield and crop margin. Establish the robot's operating costs and a reasonable upfront farmer contribution. The machine must maintain or improve crop performance while reducing the farmer's total cost.

  3. Performance standard

    Define the minimum requirements for weed-control efficacy, crop safety, field capacity, reliability and displacement of the specified herbicide application. Agree in advance how these results will be independently tested.

  4. Funded purchase commitments

    Farmers sign conditional purchase commitments stating their upfront contributions. The public fund reserves its contribution up to a fixed cap. The technology company sets the machine price. If the combined funding does not cover it, the purchase does not proceed.

  5. Delivery and finance

    Confirm that the technology company can build, deliver and support the machines. Establish the production-finance route, legal vehicle, procurement treatment and state-aid compliance.

Decision rule. Proceed only when a specific use case, viable farmer economics, validated performance requirements, funded purchase commitments and an executable delivery route are all in place.

Deep dive

Make the choice

Reliable, cheap, and the source of the exceedance.

Treats the whole field without extra labour or machinery. The substance may be the one the water board measures above its standard. Product approvals are being withdrawn, and each withdrawal removes a rescue option. Sectors report early weed control in inulin chicory became difficult after benfluralin was lost.

No chemicals, many labour hours, and labour is scarce.

Mechanical control depends on soil and weather and does not yet fully replace chemicals in every situation, especially weeds close to the crop plant. Early mechanical work is limited in herbs and witlof. Labour is scarce. Growers keep a chemical rescue treatment for when mechanical control is insufficient.

Possible, and the grower bears the risk of proving it.

Throughput per hectare, repeat passes, uptime in a narrow weather window, crop damage and cost per treated hectare are vendor claims until measured in the grower's crop. If the robot misses weeds, the grower loses yield this year and has more weed seed next year. Responsibility for a rescue spray is undefined.

The grower's actual buying criteria

  • Weeds controlled at the required stage
  • No unacceptable yield or crop damage
  • Enough machine capacity in the treatment window
  • Predictable cost per treated hectare
  • Clear responsibility for breakdowns, missed treatments and rescue spraying

All five must be set with growers before a technology supplier is chosen. The honest formulation: replace or materially reduce a particular herbicide while maintaining viable weed control and grower economics. Zero chemistry across a whole farm is not the requirement.
