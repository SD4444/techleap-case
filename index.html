The challenge

Herbicides pollute the water. Replacing them can raise costs and put crop performance at risk.

The water authority's view

Herbicides reach surface water through spray drift and runoff.

Water authorities are responsible for water quality but do not control how farms manage weeds. They can monitor, advise and co-fund alternatives; growers decide whether to switch.

Deep dives
The grower's view

Weed control must work on time and protect crop economics.

Technology alone is not enough. It must clear weeds on time, avoid crop damage and make economic sense. Until that is proven at commercial scale, herbicides remain the lower-risk choice. Failure can reduce current yield and increase future weed pressure.

Evidence the market can be organised

The building blocks already exist.

Public institutions already fund agricultural water-quality measures, established programmes reach growers, and agricultural sectors report a need for temporary transition support.

Technology landscape

Two robotic approaches can reduce reliance on herbicides.

Both approaches use cameras and machine vision to distinguish crops from weeds. The difference is how they kill the weed: physical removal or targeted light.

Physical removal

Principal advantages
  • No herbicide at the point of treatment
  • Immediate and observable weed removal
  • Based on established mechanical principles
Principal constraints
  • Performance varies with soil and moisture conditions
  • Tools can disturb soil or damage crop roots
  • Wear, maintenance and repeated passes affect cost

Targeted optical energy

Principal advantages
  • No herbicide at the point of treatment
  • Can target weeds close to the crop
  • Does not disturb soil or crop roots
Principal constraints
  • Requires reliable identification and precise targeting
  • Treatment speed and energy use determine field capacity
  • Dust, rain, plant cover and weed size can affect performance

From technical capability to commercial alternative

The ability to identify and kill a weed is only the starting point.

To become a commercial alternative, a robot must pass five tests. These determine whether growers will buy, manufacturers can plan production and investors will fund scale-up.

  1. Consistent control of the relevant weeds in the target crop
  2. No material crop damage or yield loss
  3. Enough hectares treated within the operating window
  4. A competitive total cost per hectare, including repeat passes
  5. Reliable operation, maintenance and seasonal support

Each crop and technology combination must be validated separately. Success in one crop does not qualify the machine for another. The programme can expand wherever performance and farmer economics are proven.

Each actor waits for evidence another must finance.

Growers want proven field economics. Technology companies need capital to build the machines that generate that proof. Investors hesitate without binding demand. The evidence needed to unlock both demand and capital therefore remains unfunded.

Coordination failure: each party waits for another to act, blocking a mutually beneficial outcome.

The missing asset is not another expression of interest.

It is underwritable demand: identified buyers, committed budgets, defined volumes and measurable performance conditions.

The intervention

Clean Weeding Advance Market Commitment

The programme combines conditional purchases with reserved public funding. Before suppliers compete, it defines volumes, performance standards, buyer contributions and the maximum public contribution. Suppliers set their own prices.

01 · Commit buyer demand

Buyers state what they will purchase.

Growers and agricultural contractors sign conditional commitments specifying machine volumes, target crops, delivery periods and maximum upfront contributions. Contractors may buy machines to serve several farms.

02 · Reserve the public contribution

Reserve a capped contribution for each qualifying purchase.

A water board, potentially supported by the province, reserves a capped contribution. It is paid when the buyer purchases a machine that has passed validation.

03 · Publish the performance standard

Set common requirements, not prices.

Publish requirements for efficacy, crop safety, field capacity, seasonal reliability, maintenance and delivery. Suppliers set their own prices; the programme sets neither the machine price nor a maximum cost per hectare.

04 · Validate and activate

Passing validation activates the purchase.

Independent testing determines whether the machine meets the standard. If it passes and the buyer and public contributions cover the supplier's price, the purchase proceeds. Otherwise, it does not.

Deep dives

The purchase mechanism

A purchase activates only when the farmer is better off.

Independent validation must show that the robot maintains or improves crop performance and lowers the farmer's total weed-control cost.

Validation condition 1Yield and crop margin ≥ current method
Validation condition 2Total farmer cost < current method

Unverified yield gains count as zero.

The farmer contribution must be affordable and economically justified.

The farmer's conditional order states the maximum affordable upfront contribution. It cannot exceed either the machine's verified economic value to the farm or the cash the farmer can invest without borrowing or weakening operations.

Techleap's role

Turn fragmented interest into financeable demand.

Techleap does not buy machines or administer public funding. It coordinates the parties, structures the transaction and produces decision-ready evidence. The three responsibilities run in parallel.

Define the conditions

Map which crops and weed-control operations each technology can address. Prioritise combinations with technical readiness, viable farm economics and a documented water-quality problem. Define what each machine must achieve for growers and the responsible water institution. Validate every combination separately.

Commit demand before validation

Growers and contractors sign conditional purchase commitments, while the public fund reserves its capped contribution. Both are committed before full validation and activate only if the tests are passed.

Bring investment forward

Technology companies use these commitments to raise capital for machine construction, field validation and production preparation. Passing validation activates the purchases.

The grower's decision

Growers risk yield and labour on an unproven alternative.

There is measured evidence of the trade-off. In a four-year crop-system trial on sandy soil at Vredepeel, Wageningen researchers compared a conventional reference with an integrated system using fewer crop-protection products.

Deep dive

Make the choice

Reliable, cheap, and the source of the exceedance.

Treats the whole field without extra labour or machinery. The substance may be the one the water board measures above its standard. Product approvals are being withdrawn, and each withdrawal removes a rescue option. Sectors report early weed control in inulin chicory became difficult after benfluralin was lost.

No chemicals, many labour hours, and labour is scarce.

Mechanical control depends on soil and weather and does not yet fully replace chemicals in every situation, especially weeds close to the crop plant. Early mechanical work is limited in herbs and witlof. Labour is scarce. Growers keep a chemical rescue treatment for when mechanical control is insufficient.

Possible, and the grower bears the risk of proving it.

Throughput per hectare, repeat passes, uptime in a narrow weather window, crop damage and cost per treated hectare are vendor claims until measured in the grower's crop. If the robot misses weeds, the grower loses yield this year and has more weed seed next year. Responsibility for a rescue spray is undefined.

The grower's actual buying criteria

  • Weeds controlled at the required stage
  • No unacceptable yield or crop damage
  • Enough machine capacity in the treatment window
  • Predictable cost per treated hectare
  • Clear responsibility for breakdowns, missed treatments and rescue spraying

All five must be set with growers before a technology supplier is chosen. The honest formulation: replace or materially reduce a particular herbicide while maintaining viable weed control and grower economics. Zero chemistry across a whole farm is not the requirement.
