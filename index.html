A demand-side intervention to stimulate the uptake of herbicide alternatives in farming.

The challenge

Herbicides pollute the water. Removing them costs growers yield and profits.

The water authority's view

Herbicides reach surface water through spray drift and runoff from treated fields.

Water authorities are responsible for regional water quality, but they do not decide how weeds are controlled on farms. They can monitor, advise and co-fund cleaner practices; the purchasing decision still sits with the grower. The authority carries the water-quality obligation without controlling the switch.

Deep dives
The grower's view

Weed control has to work within narrow operating windows and meet the crop's economic profile.

For a grower, promising technology is not enough. It must clear weeds on time, avoid crop damage and make economic sense. Until that performance is proven at commercial scale, herbicides remain the lower-risk choice. A failed treatment can cost yield today and increase weed pressure tomorrow.

  1. 8 critical weeks

    For inulin chicory, weed control during the first eight weeks determines whether the crop remains commercially viable. Growers report that inadequate control reduces both yield and quality enough to make the crop uneconomic.

    Source: Dutch government's 2025 crop-protection bottleneck annex, pp. 1 to 2 ↗
  2. No viable alternative

    For many crops such as brassica, herbs and witlof, hand-weeding is too expensive and labour is too scarce. Mechanical treatment often cannot begin until 10 to 14 days after planting, leaving growers exposed when early weed control matters most.

    Source: Dutch government's 2025 crop-protection bottleneck annex, pp. 1 to 2 ↗
  3. Only hours to act

    Mechanical weeding depends on soil moisture, weather and crop stage. The workable window can shrink to only a few hours: too wet and the soil smears; too dry and weeds survive or young crops are damaged.

    Source: Landbouwportaal Rijnland, mechanical weeding coach ↗

Technology landscape

Two robotic approaches can reduce reliance on herbicides.

Robotic weeders use cameras and machine vision to distinguish crops from weeds, then treat individual plants. The main technical difference is how they kill the weed: physical removal or targeted optical energy.

Physical removal

Principal advantages
  • No herbicide at the point of treatment
  • Immediate and observable weed removal
  • Based on established mechanical principles
Principal constraints
  • Performance varies with soil and moisture conditions
  • Tools can disturb soil or damage crop roots
  • Wear, maintenance and repeated passes affect cost

Targeted optical energy

Principal advantages
  • No herbicide at the point of treatment
  • Can target weeds close to the crop
  • Does not disturb soil or crop roots
Principal constraints
  • Requires reliable identification and precise targeting
  • Treatment speed and energy use determine field capacity
  • Dust, rain, plant cover and weed size can affect performance

From technical capability to commercial alternative

The ability to identify and kill a weed is only the starting point.

To become a credible alternative to herbicide-based weed control, a robotic system must demonstrate all five. Performance against these measures determines whether growers can order with confidence, manufacturers can plan production and investors can finance commercial scale-up.

  1. Consistent efficacy in the target crop and against the relevant weeds
  2. No material increase in crop damage or yield loss
  3. Sufficient field capacity within the available operating window
  4. A competitive total cost per hectare, including repeat passes
  5. Reliable operation, maintenance and technical support throughout the season

Each actor waits for evidence that another must finance.

Growers will not commit at scale until field performance and economics are proven. Technology companies cannot prove them without financing and building commercial machines. Investors hesitate while customer demand remains non-binding.

The evidence that would unlock demand and capital can only be produced through deployments that neither side will finance independently.

Coordination failure: when economic agents fail to align their decisions, leaving everyone in a suboptimal or worse-off state even though a mutually beneficial outcome is possible.

The missing asset is not another expression of interest.

It is underwritable demand: identified buyers, committed budgets, defined volumes and measurable performance conditions.

The intervention

Clean weeding Advance Market Commitment

Growers commit to purchase machines that meet a defined commercial standard. A public water-quality fund reserves a temporary contribution toward the purchase price. Suppliers receive no blank cheque: the orders become binding only when independent field validation confirms that the machine works.

01 · Fund the commitment

A fully funded conditional order book, before a supplier is selected.

Growers, cooperatives or agricultural contractors sign conditional orders for a defined number of machines, at a stated price and delivery date.

The public participant reserves a capped budget for the clean premium. Together, the buyer contribution and public contribution create a fully funded conditional order book before a supplier is selected.

02 · Publish the commercial standard

All suppliers compete against the same outcome-based specification.

  • Weed-control efficacy in the selected crop
  • Maximum crop damage and yield impact
  • Field capacity within the operating window
  • Seasonal uptime and service response
  • Total cost per hectare, including repeat passes
  • Displacement of the specified herbicide application

The commitment remains technology-neutral between robotic mechanical, light and laser systems.

03 · Validate once, independently

Qualified suppliers receive paid contracts for a controlled field-validation season.

The test protocol, baseline, acceptance thresholds, rescue-treatment rules and verification method are fixed in advance. Performance is assessed by an independent technical party, not by the supplier or prospective buyer.

04 · Convert automatically into purchase agreements

Passing the test activates a predefined allocation of orders.

Grower commitments become firm, and the reserved public contribution becomes payable.

The resulting advance purchase agreement specifies units, price, delivery schedule, warranties, service obligations and remedies for underperformance. Suppliers that fail do not receive scale orders.

05 · Finance and manufacture

Financing is based on contracted customers, not demonstrations.

The manufacturer takes the signed order book, validation results and secured payment structure to equity, working-capital and equipment-finance providers.

Investors still bear production and execution risk. What changes is the evidence of demand: financing is now based on contracted customers rather than demonstrations and non-binding interest.

Evidence that the mechanism can work

Gavi Pneumococcal AMC

A $1.5 billion commitment combined product requirements, an initial price premium and long-term supply agreements. Manufacturers expanded production capacity; 1.75 billion doses were contracted through 2029, and the long-term price ultimately fell 43%.

Sources: Kremer, Levin and Snyder, 2020 ↗; Gavi, Pneumococcal AMC ↗

AgResults Kenya

Verified sales incentives for agricultural storage equipment generated 1.39 million device sales. $6.25 million of incentives was accompanied by more than $4.85 million of supplier investment.

Source: AgResults, Kenya on-farm storage project ↗

Research and decision gates

Seven facts to establish before execution.

Each gate has a concrete owner and a concrete output. Several can run in parallel. None can be skipped by citing a national figure.

  1. Crop × substance × catchment

    A crop using a named herbicide found above the water-quality limit in its catchment, and a robot that can plausibly displace that application. The Vredepeel pain and the Rijnland exceedances are different pieces of evidence; the overlap has to be shown.

  2. Real farmer economics

    Grower invoices or interviews on current weed-control spend, yield exposure, manual hours, rescue treatments, and willingness to sign a conditional purchase at a specific price.

  3. Technology readiness

    Supplier field records: efficacy, repeat passes, hours per hectare, uptime, weather limits, crop damage, fuel or power use, manufacturing cost. Not marketing claims.

  4. Contract strength

    Buyer, enforceable minimum volume, price, conditions, cancellation rights, credit risk. Ask two relevant investors whether this changes their actual underwriting, and on what terms.

  5. Water-board anchor and additionality

    A specified conditional budget for a target catchment. Verify with the board and counsel which funding route, how long, how material, and how it interacts with existing subsidies and state-aid limits. If no public contribution is secured, the order must work on private economics alone.

  6. Novelty

    Check what Roboltics, Cropr, Odd.Bot, current growers, lessors and agricultural water programmes already offer. The mechanism must create commitments or financing outcomes those channels do not.

  7. Scope fit

    Lead with Techleap's deep-tech commercialisation and capital-activation remit. Ask whether water quality, nature and agricultural robotics fit the Climate Tech or deep-tech mandate. No emissions promise without lifecycle analysis.

Decision rule. If no buyer will contract for meaningful future volume at a usable price, even with technical conditions clearly written, this is not yet a demand-creation mechanism. If buyers sign and investors still do not find the orders meaningful, the constraint is machine economics, production reliability or market size, and the case must be re-examined.

Deep dive

Make the choice

Reliable, cheap, and the source of the exceedance.

Treats the whole field without extra labour or machinery. The substance may be the one the water board measures above its standard. Product approvals are being withdrawn, and each withdrawal removes a rescue option. Sectors report early weed control in inulin chicory became difficult after benfluralin was lost.

No chemicals, many labour hours, and labour is scarce.

Mechanical control depends on soil and weather and does not yet fully replace chemicals in every situation, especially weeds close to the crop plant. Early mechanical work is limited in herbs and witlof. Labour is scarce. Growers keep a chemical rescue treatment for when mechanical control is insufficient.

Possible, and the grower bears the risk of proving it.

Throughput per hectare, repeat passes, uptime in a narrow weather window, crop damage and cost per treated hectare are vendor claims until measured in the grower's crop. If the robot misses weeds, the grower loses yield this year and has more weed seed next year. Responsibility for a rescue spray is undefined.

The grower's actual buying criteria

  • Weeds controlled at the required stage
  • No unacceptable yield or crop damage
  • Enough machine capacity in the treatment window
  • Predictable cost per treated hectare
  • Clear responsibility for breakdowns, missed treatments and rescue spraying

All five must be set with growers before a technology supplier is chosen. The honest formulation: replace or materially reduce a particular herbicide while maintaining viable weed control and grower economics. Zero chemistry across a whole farm is not the requirement.
